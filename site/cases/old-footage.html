The footage that was two years old

On the morning of 2 June 2026 a clip of the river Ostra over its banks in Lindmark spreads under the caption “JUST IN”. A newsroom's tip line holds 36 clips from the same days, many of them brown water under grey skies, and has to say which ones are new.

Fictional case of verification. The town, the river, the accounts, the captions and the dates are invented. The footage is real, openly licensed and filmed elsewhere.

Watch this case in the film, from 1:59

The story

The viral clip carries a red banner and a logo block and runs 14 seconds. Put beside the older clips in the pile, its picture matches none of them.

It is a cropped, mirrored, trimmed and re-encoded copy of a video posted on 3 June 2024. The picture no longer matches the original. The sound still does.

What it shows

  • Listening to and looking at every clip
  • Search by what a clip shows
  • Sound and picture fingerprints
  • Two clips side by side
  • A conclusion that cites a confirmed match

The pile

36 clips of real footage: one 21 second original, nine copies of it, six other real recordings of rivers in flood posted on the same days with similar captions, and twenty unrelated clips of clouds, rain, snow, waterfalls, a rail line and a fountain.

Day by day

  1. 2 June 2026

    The clip that spread

    Arrives
    The viral clip, with the post it came in.
    The app
    Listens for speech, looks at eight frames and proposes a title, a description and labels for what the clip shows. The caption stays what the post reports, unconfirmed.
  2. 2 June

    Reposts and other floods

    Arrives
    20 more clips over the day: reposts, other people's flood videos and clips that only look like weather.
    The app
    Reads each one and fingerprints its picture and its sound. Several reposts match each other by sound, so one recording is spreading. Nothing yet says where it began.
  3. 3 June

    Searching the pile by what it shows

    Arrives
    14 more clips in one batch.
    A person
    Searches for what the viral clip shows, submerged trees, instead of scrolling 35 videos. The app's own descriptions find 8 of the 9 copies and one other flood. A description says what the frames show, never where or when they were filmed.
  4. 3 June

    The archive from two years ago

    Arrives
    A producer remembers a flood clip from June 2024, and the desk adds Ostra Valley Weather's video from the archive.
    The app
    Its fingerprints tie the 2024 video to the viral clip and six other reposts by sound, two of them by picture too. The search for submerged trees now finds it first.
  5. 3 June

    Side by side

    A person
    Plays the viral clip beside the 2024 video, hears the same water and sees the same bush mirrored. She confirms each of the 29 matches as the same recording, with her reason and her passkey. The app proposed nothing between clips that are not the same footage, so there is nothing to decline.
  6. 3 June

    The conclusion

    A person
    Writes that the clip shared as new is the 2024 video and cites the confirmed matches beside the two posts' words. She signs it; a second analyst agrees.

The conclusion

The score

8 of 9 planted copies tied to the original.

All eight are banded strong, and no match of any strength falls outside the planted family. Sound did most of the work: the logo, the crop, the mirror and an upright cut defeat the picture fingerprint, so five of the eight were found by sound alone. A muted copy of the viral clip matched it by picture, and through it the original.

Measured on 22 November 2026 on this synthetic case by a scoring script that runs the app's fingerprint code over the 36 clips, with exact search in place of the database. A test in the source pins the result.

The limit

A copy cut upright, mirrored and with its sound removed is not found. The picture fingerprint does not survive a crop or a mirror, and with no sound left there is nothing to match. A sound match needs four points lined up, so two clips that share less than about a second of sound are not proposed by sound. And the speech model heard "subscribe to the channel" in running water on two clips; the first pass then listed it as said, and a person has to catch that.

From finding to court

Every approval in the app is a passkey signature by a named person, and every decision can be undone in the open. A signed finding leaves in a sealed package. Anyone handed that package, a court included, can check offline that not one word changed since it was signed, without trusting you or us.
