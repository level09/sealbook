Sealbook helps investigators turn raw evidence into findings they can defend.

Evidence intelligence for human rights teams who document detention, disappearance and attacks from photos, videos, documents, spreadsheets and testimony. AI reads every file and the case around it and proposes what it finds, each claim pinned to the exact words, page, cell or second it came from. People make every factual decision.

Investigate with AI
Extraction, search, transcription and agents that read whole cases and keep watching them, all proposing, never deciding.
Verify with people
Every fact is accepted, rejected or corrected by a named reviewer with a passkey, and every decision can be undone.
Keep every source
Files are fingerprinted on arrival, never overwritten, and disagreements between them stay open.
Prove it offline
Findings leave as a signed package that anyone can check offline with the bundled verifier, without having to trust you or us.

      A messy sheet

      Messy intake lists, read without guessing.

      Lists from the field mix Arabic and Latin, write dates several ways and put several facts in one cell. Sealbook does not guess what a column means: a person names each one first. Every fact it then reads points back to its exact cell. When a newer version of the list arrives, only new and changed rows are read again.

      How it works

      Intake lists arrive as spreadsheets with mixed scripts, dates in several forms and several facts in one cell. Sealbook stores the sheet as evidence and reads nothing until a person names a field for every column. Then each row is read, and every fact comes back as a proposal pinned to its cell, down to the characters.

      A later version of the same list is compared with the last one first, row by row, and only added and changed rows are read again.

      A batch, read as a whole

      A summary of what just arrived, and what to do next.

      When a batch of files is in, two assistants read it against the rest of the case. One writes a short brief: who and where it mentions, what it connects to, what contradicts facts you already accepted, and up to five next steps. The other suggests links, such as the same person in two sources, and sends each to Review as a question.

      They only suggest. A sentence they cannot back with a source is thrown out. They run locally, so sensitive material never leaves the machine.

      How it works

      When a batch finishes, two agents read it against the rest of the case. The intake brief says what arrived, who and where it names, when, what it connects to and what disagrees with accepted facts, then suggests up to five next steps, each opening the screen where that work is done. The linker proposes links the claims support: one person in two sources, a person at an incident's place and time, two documents naming the same people, people held together. Each link reaches Review as a question, with the claims it rests on.

      Agents only propose. A sentence the model writes without a citation, or citing something it was not given, is dropped. A line resting on a sensitive source stays locked until someone makes a logged reveal. Both run on a local model, so sensitive material never leaves the machine.

      One search, grouped

      Ask once, find it in any language or format.

      Type a name and get people, places, documents, passages, photos and recordings in one view. Exact matches come first, in any spelling or script the sources used. Matches by meaning follow, marked as leads. A written question can even find a picture or a recording. Every result opens at the exact word, page or second. Tested on synthetic data so far.

      How it works

      One query returns people, places, sources, passages, media and claims, each in its own group. Exact matches come first: a name in any recorded spelling, a place in either script, an order number, a file hash. Matches by meaning follow as leads, labelled close, related or loose, and leads far below the best match are left out. Every result opens at the character, page or second it came from.

      One local model places text, pictures, scanned pages and audio in the same space, so a written question can find an image or a recording. It has been measured on synthetic data only, where scanned pages rank weakest.

      Recordings are transcribed on the machine that holds them, so sensitive audio never leaves it. Each transcript line keeps its start and end time and is indexed like page text, so a claim drawn from it opens the recording at that second.

      Same footage, different sources

      Know when ten uploads are really one photo.

      The same picture gets shared, shrunk and posted again. Sealbook recognises copies by what they look and sound like, not by file name, so one recording is never counted as several witnesses. It also shows where it fails: a cropped copy is not caught. Nothing is linked until a person confirms it.

      How it works

      Re-encoded and resized copies of a photo are found by picture fingerprint. A short audio excerpt is found by sound landmarks, with the time it was cut from recovered. Cropping is different: a cropped copy moves past the fingerprint threshold and is not caught, and the demo shows that too. A distance is not a probability, and nothing is linked until a person confirms it.

      One street, two years apart

      Find the same moment across re-uploads.

      Two real, openly licensed clips of the Jdeideh quarter in Aleppo, from April 2015 and September 2017. Sealbook checks them a second at a time, by picture and by sound, and finds re-uploads even when they are trimmed or carry a logo. Cropped and mirrored copies fool the picture check, but their soundtrack still gives them away. You can also search the footage with a written question in English or Arabic. Every match is a suggestion for a person to check.

      How it works

      Two real, openly licensed clips of the Jdeideh quarter in Aleppo: an explosion over its rooftops in April 2015, and the same quarter in ruins in September 2017. Sealbook samples a frame every second, fingerprints each one, and matches re-uploaded copies frame by frame and by sound, recovering where in the original each copy starts.

      Re-encoded, trimmed and logo-stamped copies match. Cropped and mirrored copies do not match by picture; their unchanged soundtrack still does. A written question, in English or Arabic, finds the moment in the footage. Every match here is a proposal for a person to check.

      One name, two people

      Same name is not the same person.

      The case holds two men with the same name and the same father's name, but different mothers and birth years. Sealbook shows possible matches across Arabic and Latin spellings and what supports each, but never merges two people on its own. When sources disagree about someone, every version stays visible with its source until a reviewer decides.

      How it works

      The case holds two men with the same name, the same father's name and different mothers and birth years. Sealbook proposes identity candidates across Arabic and Latin spellings, and shows what supports each one. Similar names are never merged on their own.

      Where sources disagree about a person, every side stays listed with its source until a reviewer decides. Two spellings of one place are not a disagreement, and neither is a last sighting and an arrest on the same day. A person's facts and documents are counted the same way on the People list, the person page and in search.

      Connections, with their reasons

      Patterns across the case, each with its reason.

      Sealbook looks across facts people already accepted and points out two kinds of pattern: people held or seen at one place in the same month, and several people from separate sources in one short window, which may be one incident. Each suggestion says why in one sentence, shows how strong it is and lists every fact behind it. Once you accept or reject one, it is never suggested again.

      How it works

      Discovery reads claims that are accepted or corroborated, dated and placed, and proposes two kinds of finding: people held or seen at one place in the same month, and a candidate incident when several people from independent sources fall in one short window. Two nearby places join only within the larger of their stated precisions, and the reason names the distance. Each finding says why in one sentence, carries a strength band instead of a probability, and lists every member claim with its span.

      The list is bounded per kind and investigation. Once a set has been accepted or rejected it is never proposed again, because its key covers the kind, investigation, people, places and month. Accepting is a checklist signed with one passkey gesture over every statement it writes, and that signature can be checked offline.

      A person over time

      A person's path, with the gaps and contradictions left in.

      Pick a person, group, incident or place to see the accepted facts on a timeline and a map. If sources put someone in two distant places on the same day, both are shown, not smoothed over. A month or more with no evidence is marked as a gap, and findings still under review are hatched so they are not mistaken for settled ones.

      How it works

      Pick a person, a group, an incident or a place, and the accepted claims are laid out in time order and on a map. When sources put one person in two places on one day, farther apart than the places' stated precision, the fork is shown, not resolved. A stretch of more than a month with no evidence is labelled as a gap. Findings still waiting for review appear as hatched bands.

      Every place carries a precision and says where its position came from: the gazetteer, official boundaries, or an analyst. The map numbers places in time order and joins them with lines that show sequence, not routes.

      Web research that keeps custody

      Search the web without breaking the chain of custody.

      From a person or a source, an analyst can search the open web. Sealbook suggests the searches, the analyst edits them and sees exactly what will be sent, and every search is logged before it goes out. A case marked sensitive sends nothing.

      When a result is worth keeping, Sealbook visits the page itself and saves it with a full screenshot and a record of what loaded, all fingerprinted. It enters the case as your own capture, with no third party in between.

      How it works

      From a person or a source, an analyst can search the open web through Brave. Queries are proposed only from accepted or corroborated values in ordinary sources, gazetteer names and years. The analyst edits them and sees exactly what will be sent. Each query is written to the ledger as an access record before it leaves, and a case marked sensitive sends nothing.

      Results come back as web, image and video lists with domain, date and the reason each was suggested, flagged when the same link or a matching picture is already in the case. Adding one as evidence sends a local headless browser to the page once. It stores the final address, the status, the rendered page, a full-page screenshot and a log of what loaded, each hashed, and the files enter normal intake as the analyst's acquisition, so no third party sits in the chain of custody. A login form, a bot check or an error page fails the capture in plain words.

      Review together

      Check each other's work, and sign every decision.

      Ask a colleague to check a fact, or hand over a whole set of sources. Requests and replies land in one inbox, and the desk shows what changed while you were away. Before you sign a decision you see exactly what you are signing, and any decision can be undone, also with a signature.       How it works

      Ask a colleague to check a claim, or assign a whole selection of sources at once. The discussion stays with the people in it and does not appear in general views, history or exports.       Proof

      Show, years from now, that not one word was changed.

      Every decision your team makes is sealed, like an evidence bag with a tamper strip. Sealbook then folds thousands of those seals into one short code. Change a single word in a single decision and that code no longer matches.

      Give the code to a timestamp service or a partner archive, and anyone can later check your findings against it. They do not need to trust you, and they do not need to trust us.

      Prove one finding without handing over the rest. A prosecutor given one statement can check it against the code with a short list of fingerprints, offline, without seeing anything else in the archive.

      How it works

      The seals are SHA-256 hashes. Pairs of them are hashed together, level by level, into a Merkle tree, and the last hash left is the root. An exported package carries, for each cited statement, only the sibling hashes on its path to the root, and the bundled verifier rebuilds the root from those.

      Statements are grouped into Merkle batches. One root stands for the whole batch, and any change to any statement changes the root. The app can request timestamp receipts for a root and copy it to partner archives, and an exported package of cited findings can be verified offline with the bundled verifier.

      Those receipt and partner paths are implemented, but this is not a configured or certified deployment. Erasure removes a person's values from views and search while the chain still verifies. It excludes access; it does not physically delete stored bytes.

      What we learned

      1. One source is not two. Several uploads of one recording are one origin. Corroboration counts only after a person confirms they are the same recording, and undoing that link changes the tiers back.
      2. Similar names are not one person. Embeddings and fuzzy matches propose. They never merge identities.
      3. Two spellings are not a disagreement. Places compare by the place they resolve to and names by normalised text, never by how they are displayed, or "Aleppo" and "حلب" look like a contradiction.
      4. A count on two screens needs one definition. The People list once counted identity links as facts and disagreed with the person page. Both read one rule, and a screen check compares the two.
      5. An agent's citations go in the citation field. A model asked to cite will write claim handles into its prose. Any sentence that still carries one is dropped.
      6. Erasure must reach every copy. Search entries, prepared media and anything that shares bytes with the erased source all have to be blocked, or the value leaks back through a side door.
      7. A model saying "done" does not mean it looked. One endpoint answered HTTP 200 while silently ignoring the media it was sent. Distinct test images catch that; a success code does not.
      8. Search must cite the original text. Arabic is normalized to find matches, but the citation points back to the exact characters in the stored evidence.
      9. Private means private everywhere. Hiding a discussion in the inbox was not enough. It also had to be kept out of history, reveals and exports.
      10. A forgiving test can hide a broken tool. A loose threshold can make a broken matcher look right. The audio test asserts the known time offset of an excerpt, not just that something matched.
